## Introduction

Since the existence of humans, curiosity has driven the whole civilization to quest after every unusual happening. Whatever new happens is viewed with a question: why did it happen? It was such questions that turned Homo sapiens into the intelligent humans of the present day. In Physics we study various phenomena of nature to know: how does Nature behave? In the long course of time, we have studied many of them and found that nature always follows certain laws. Most of the time, we discover a law which is less accurate and can be applied only to some part of the physical world. Thereafter we correlate two or more laws to construct a more general form of those laws, which can be applied to a wide range of observed facts. Hence we reform our ideas, and this process of reformation has been going on since the evolution of the human race. Let's have a glimpse of these reforms.

## Motion of objects: Understood

The description of the geocentric world, which was first given by Plato and then Ptolemy, dominated our understanding till Copernicus gave us the idea that the Sun is the centre of the solar system. Galileo devoted much to our understanding of the motion of objects, on which Newton built his famous three laws. Kepler, using the data of Brahe, gave his three laws of planetary motion. Later on, Lagrange and Hamilton showed a better way of expressing the equations of motion.

## Understanding light

Light emitted from the Sun travels through vacuum and reaches the Earth; it also shows effects like reflection and refraction. Snell in 1621 discovered the law of refraction experimentally, and then in 1637 Descartes published his derivation of Snell's law, which was equivalent to the corpuscular theory of light. This theory explained reflection and refraction as well as dispersion, and hence we believed light to be a stream of corpuscles. But effects like interference, diffraction and polarization could not be explained on the basis of the corpuscular model of light. Huygens in 1679 proposed the wave model of light, but no one really believed in this theory until Young performed his interference experiment in 1801, which could only be explained on the basis of wave theory. As a wave requires a medium for its propagation, we assumed an 'ether' which we thought pervaded all space, and hence the wave theory was well established. Until 1849, when Fizeau measured the velocity of light as 3.14858 × 10⁸ m/s, we believed the velocity of light to be infinite.

On the other side, Maxwell, from his famous equations, predicted the existence of electromagnetic waves. Later on, in 1856, using the data of Kohlrausch and Weber, he found the velocity of electromagnetic waves in air to be 3.107 × 10⁸ m/s, which was very close to the velocity of light. Hence it was then believed that light waves are electromagnetic waves, and towards the end of the nineteenth century we thought that we had understood light. But again a problem arose when Hertz discovered the photoelectric effect in 1887, which could not be explained using wave theory.

At the end of the nineteenth century, it was believed that Newtonian mechanics, kinetic theory, Maxwell's electromagnetic equations and some other so-called 'classical theories' were capable of explaining a wide range of natural phenomena. Yes, there were certain problems yet to be solved, but it was felt that these would soon be explained using all the known theories.

## Rise of Modern Physics

One of the problems was to explain black body radiation. Although Maxwell's theory predicted that radiation was due to oscillating charges, it was incapable of predicting the observed spectrum. The two classical theories, namely Wien's theory and the Rayleigh-Jeans theory, made unsuccessful attempts to explain the whole spectrum. Wien's theory was applicable at shorter wavelengths and inapplicable at longer wavelengths, whereas the Rayleigh-Jeans theory explained longer wavelengths but not the shorter ones. In 1900 Max Planck revolutionized our idea about nature by proposing that energy is not a continuous quantity, as we believed; rather, it is quantized. Planck's quantum hypothesis gave an empirical formula that nicely explained the whole spectrum of black body radiation.

Following Planck's idea that energy transfers in the form of 'quanta', Einstein proposed an explanation of the photoelectric effect, which called the corpuscles of light 'photons'. Einstein also published the 'Special Theory of Relativity', which is considered one of the greatest achievements of mankind.

## Matter magnified

In another realm, it was well established by then that matter is made up of a fundamental entity called the atom, and that the whole world is constituted by a finite number of basic elements. It was believed that the atom was a fundamental particle without any internal structure, but some experiments questioned this belief. Henri Becquerel discovered that uranium gave out a type of radiation that could affect photographic plates, ionize gases and cause scintillations. Pierre Curie and Marie Curie extracted radium from pitchblende. Rutherford and his co-workers discovered alpha and beta radiation; later, Villard discovered gamma radiation. J. J. Thomson, trying to explain the internal structure of the atom, proposed that electrons and protons are symmetrically located in the atom. When Geiger and Marsden performed the experiment of α-particle scattering from thin gold foil, Rutherford found that very few particles deflected through large angles, and instead of treating it as an error, it was Rutherford's genius to question the one in ten thousand that came back. This led him to his model, which proposed a heavy, positively charged nucleus located at the centre with electrons around it. But it gave rise to the instability of the atom, hence Rutherford proposed electrons orbiting around the nucleus. Again, this led to a serious difficulty from the point of view of electromagnetic theory, as a revolving electron should radiate energy continuously and consequently fall into the nucleus. Bohr, to overcome the difficulties of the Rutherford atom, proposed that electrons do not radiate energy while moving in 'stationary orbits'; rather, they radiate when they move from an excited state to a stationary state. This was a big leap from the classical to the quantum world.

In the year 1911 superconductivity was discovered by Kamerlingh Onnes; he showed that current flowed with zero resistance through liquid He under specific conditions. C. T. R. Wilson devised an instrument known as the cloud chamber, by which it became possible to detect charged particles. In 1923, Compton explained the scattering of short-wavelength light by electrons using photon theory, which provided a firm foundation for the photon theory of light. Hence we believe that radiation sometimes behaves as a wave and sometimes as a particle, but not as both simultaneously; consequently, we accepted wave-particle duality.

## The rise of Quantum Theory

In 1924 de Broglie proposed that if light behaves like particles and waves, then matter may also have wavelike properties, which was later confirmed by the electron diffraction experiment of Davisson and Germer. The same year, Satyendra Nath Bose found a way to derive Planck's equation for black body radiation using a statistical approach, and with Einstein he developed the rules followed by the particles categorized, after his name, as 'bosons'. Wolfgang Pauli gave the idea of 'spin', which can only point up or down. The next year Pauli gave the exclusion principle, which prevents any two electrons from occupying the same state. Heisenberg developed matrix mechanics in 1925, the first complete, self-consistent theory of quantum mechanics. Fermi and Dirac gave their statistics, followed by 'fermions', and Schrödinger formulated wave mechanics later, in 1926. Dirac showed that both Heisenberg's and Schrödinger's mechanics are special cases of his operator theory. Meanwhile, Dirac succeeded in obtaining a relativistic version of quantum mechanics. He proposed a relativistic wave equation from which emerged properties like the spin and the magnetic moment of the electron, and above all it introduced for the first time in Physics the concepts of the antiparticle, pair creation and annihilation, and the vacuum of elementary particles.

While studying the motion of macroscopic bodies it is quite easy to measure position and momentum simultaneously, but Heisenberg showed by his famous uncertainty principle that in the case of quantum particles it is impossible to measure position and momentum simultaneously. In 1929 he and Pauli introduced the Lagrangian version of quantum mechanics. Hence the shift from classical to quantum was equally a shift from a deterministic view to a probabilistic view, from certainty to uncertainty, and from continuity to discreteness.

After the discovery of radioactivity, much effort was made to understand the internal structure of the nucleus and the rays emitted by radioactive nuclei. W. Pauli put forward the concept of the neutrino in 1930 to account for the apparent loss of energy in the process of nuclear beta decay. Later, E. Fermi developed the theory of beta decay, incorporating Pauli's idea of the neutrino.

## New era of Particle Physics

In 1932 Chadwick discovered the neutron, and after his discovery it was established beyond doubt that the nucleus consists of protons and neutrons. In 1933 Carl Anderson discovered the antiparticle of the electron, called the 'positron'. In an effort to understand short-range nuclear forces, Yukawa proposed that the nuclear forces between nucleons are the result of the exchange of charged mesons. Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann discovered nuclear fission in 1939. Three years later, Enrico Fermi achieved the first controlled nuclear chain reaction. An example depicting the power of conservation laws was the observation of the neutrino, a long 26 years after its prediction, by C. L. Cowan, F. Reines and collaborators.

Early in 1947, while measuring the spacing between energy levels for the various quantum states of the electron in the hydrogen atom, Willis Lamb and Robert Retherford found that although Dirac's theory predicts identical energies for two states of hydrogen, they have a small difference. This showed that Dirac's theory was incomplete; the cause was the 'self-interaction' of the electron, which seemed to become infinite in Dirac's theory. Then Feynman, using path integrals and diagrams called 'Feynman diagrams', applied his ideas to this problem, and using renormalization the problem of infinite self-interaction was solved. Schwinger also independently obtained equivalent results. This theory was Quantum Electrodynamics (QED), which showed that the interaction between two charged particles occurs by the exchange of photons. QED predicted values, such as the electron's magnetic moment, to great accuracy.

## Towards Unification

Today we believe in four types of fundamental forces in nature: strong, weak, electromagnetic and gravitational. Electromagnetic forces result from the exchange of photons, and weak forces are a consequence of W± and Z⁰ particle exchange, whereas the quantum of the gravitational force is called the 'graviton', which is yet to be identified. Similarly, QCD, the theory explaining the strong force, indicates that forces between quarks act through the exchange of gluons.

The first example of a unification of forces was Maxwell's electromagnetic theory. It not only brought electric and magnetic fields together but was also Lorentz invariant. Einstein hoped to unify the electromagnetic and gravitational forces, but was unsuccessful. Around the 1960s, S. Weinberg, S. Glashow and A. Salam put forward a theory which says that the electromagnetic force and the weak force are different manifestations of the electroweak interaction. Electroweak theory successfully predicted the W± and Z⁰ particles and their masses with excellent agreement. Then attempts were made to incorporate electroweak theory and the strong force in a Grand Unified Theory. In the mid 1980s, superstring theory attempted to incorporate gravity and the Grand Unified Theory into a single theory, whose elementary particles are imagined to be one-dimensional strings about 10⁻³⁵ m long. Well, such modern theories are difficult and expensive to test experimentally.

## Conclusion

Thus our ideas about nature changed drastically with each and every development over the centuries. Though we have travelled such a long way to understand nature, we have not yet understood its behaviour. So the reformation goes on and on.
