To a casual observer, the Sun, the Moon, planets, stars, comets, etc. all appear to rise from the east and set in the west. A very naive explanation for this would be, that all of these objects were stuck onto the inside of a huge sphere that rotates around the Earth. Since they all appear to be at the same distance from Earth, it was natural for the early thinkers and natural philosophers, to believe that the Earth is at the centre of the universe. To us, it seems obvious that the Earth revolves around the Sun because that is what everyone believes now. But when men like Nicolaus Copernicus, Johannes Kepler, Galileo Galilei and Isaac Newton, first proposed a heliocentric model, they were ridiculed and even persecuted, for challenging the existing dogma. This is the story of a man, who diligently observed the night sky for over 20 years, and kept meticulous records, of the positions of stars and planets. The unparalleled accuracy of his data allowed people like Kepler, and Newton to build a mathematical model, that not only proved the heliocentric view of the universe but also provided the foundations on which our current understanding of the universe is built. Our understanding of the universe and more specifically the solar system has evolved drastically in the past 2000 years. The present model of the universe is completely different than the model proposed by Ptolemy and Aristotle. This has only been made possible by people like the eccentric, but brilliant astronomer, Tycho Brahe, who set new standards for precise and objective measurements that, allowed others to build a more accurate model of the universe.

Tycho was born on 14th December 1546 in the then Danish province of, Scania. At the age of two, he was adopted by his uncle. His uncle wished to groom him as a statesman, and sent Tycho off to the University of Copenhagen, at the age of 12, to study law and other subjects suited for such a role. In those days, it was customary for students to enter universities at a much earlier age, so this is not to be taken as a sign of Tycho's genius. Tycho's real genius lay in carrying out accurate measurements of the celestial bodies and in devising instruments for doing this. At Copenhagen, Tycho became interested in astronomy and started devoting more and more time to observing the motions of the heavenly bodies. In 1560, Tycho experienced a partial solar eclipse and was intrigued by the fact that such an event could be actually predicted, though the prediction was off by a day. Driven by his desire to better understand how such a prediction could be made, he set out to procure as many books on astronomy, he could find. This included an ephemeris that, lists the calculated positions of various heavenly bodies at different times.

After about three years, in Copenhagen, his uncle decided to send him on a study tour across Europe, thinking this would draw young Tycho's attention away from the stars to more useful subjects. To ensure that Tycho focused on his studies, his uncle appointed a young man named Vedel to tutor and mentor him. Accordingly, Vedel and his student set out for the University of Leipzig, in 1562. However, Vedel was not successful in weaning Tycho away from his study of the stars. He spent all of the time he could spare studying the motion of the stars, using instruments that he built himself; and all his money to buy books on astronomy.

Soon Tycho started noticing that the actual positions of the planets differed widely from that predicted by the most accurate ephemeris then available. Young Tycho realized that the only way to fix this discrepancy was to thoroughly investigate the movements of the planets, by carrying out a protracted series of measurements over a period of years, with the most accurate instruments possible. His first instrument was just a simple pair of callipers, with which he measured the angles. He later moved on to using a contrivance known as a cross-staff, which is just a long rod with markings, and a smaller rod that can slide up and down, while held perpendicular to the long rod. To measure the angle between two objects, the cross-staff is held such that, the tips of the small rod touch the objects under observation, and the length from the tip of the long rod, to the smaller rod measured. This length can then be used to calculate the angle between the objects. The real genius of Tycho was that he realized the inevitable errors that would arise from various factors, such as workmanship, and the skill in wielding the instrument, and recorded the probable error margins as well. This allowed him to further refine and calibrate his instruments, against existing instruments. The other important thing he did was to maintain journals with very detailed observation logs.

Since the days of Plato and Aristotle, the prevailing belief was that the Earth is at the centre of the universe, with the Moon, the Sun, the planets and stars stuck onto transparent spheres that revolved around the earth. Astronomers had long studied the motion of planets, with respect to the stars, at different times of the year. This shift in the apparent position of an object, with respect to objects in the background, is known as parallax. An object that is further away from the earth will show a smaller shift in position with respect to the stars. On the contrary, the closer an object is to Earth, the larger, will be the parallax angle. According to the proponents of the Aristotelian model, all heavenly bodies resided on different spheres, based on the observed parallax. The Moon was on the closest celestial sphere, with only the Terrestrial sphere lying between the Earth and the Moon. The prevailing dictum was that the world beyond the orbit of the moon was unchangeable. A corollary to this axiom is that any drastic changes seen in the sky should be happening on the terrestrial sphere, between the Earth and the Moon.

An event that seriously challenged this Aristotelian worldview, happened on the night of November 11th in 1572. Tycho happened to observe a star in the constellation of Cassiopeia, which just showed up from nowhere. The star was even brighter than the mighty Venus, which is usually the brightest object in the sky, after the Sun and the Moon. This intrigued Tycho, who gave all his attention to this newly appeared star and observed it daily for a few months, measuring its position, and parallax, against the background of the stars. He was astonished to find, that the parallax of this star was even smaller as compared to planets like Saturn, which had a barely detectable parallax. This observation led him to disprove Aristotle's axiom that the world beyond the Moon is static. He published this discovery in a book in 1573, called "De Nova Stella", which made him famous across Europe. This discovery launched him on the path, that would make him a famous astronomer.

The fame of Tycho is now widespread, the King of Denmark, invited him to deliver a course of lectures on Astronomy at Copenhagen. Desirous of keeping the now famous Dane in Denmark, and as a reward for some work that he had done as an agent of the King, Tycho was granted the island of Hven and the funds he needed to build an observatory, by King Frederick II. Here he laid the foundation of "The Castle of Urania", Uraniborg, dedicated to the Muse of Astronomy. This was the first custom-built astronomical observatory in modern Europe. At Uraniborg, Tycho designed and built several large instruments to accurately observe the positions of planets and stars. The large size of these instruments helped him to overcome the limitations of the current tools, by reducing the error margin, and thus provided him with better accuracy. As the fame of Tycho and the observatory at Hven increased, people flocked to Hven to study under Tycho. At one time he had around 100 people working or studying at Uraniborg. When Tycho realized that various external factors, such as weather, affected the accuracy of his instruments, he constructed another underground observatory close by to reduce those errors.

Tycho went on to make some more observations that challenged the Aristotelian views. A notable one being, The Great Comet of 1577, which was reportedly first observed by Tycho. He made countless sketches and very precise measurements of the comet's position. On carefully comparing his observations with that of another astronomer in Prague, that was made at the same time, he found that the parallax exhibited by the comet was less than that of the Moon. Hence the comet could not have originated on the terrestrial sphere, as previously believed.

Interestingly though Tycho admired Copernicus's work, and was one of the first to teach the Copernican theory in Europe, he still believed the Copernican to be wrong. This was mainly because nobody was able to offer a viable explanation, on how a massive body like the Earth could move through space. Moreover, he believed that the observational data, that Copernicus used to derive his model had a high margin of error. Hence even though his own observations, showed flaws in the Aristotelian model, he still considered the Aristotelian model as a foundation to build on.

After the death of King Frederick II, Tycho no longer enjoyed royal patronage, and had to leave Hven, and move to Prague, with just his data, and a few instruments. In Prague, Tycho along with Johannes Kepler, set about to work on creating a more accurate ephemeris and star catalogue, listing over 1000 stars. This work resulted in the creation of the "Rudolphine Tables", which is perhaps the most accurate star catalogue of that time.

In his lifetime, Tycho played many roles, but what he will always be remembered for, is the extremely accurate measurements he made. What makes his work noteworthy, is that his observations were all made with extremely rudimentary instruments, by modern standards. He did not even have a very simple telescope, which modern astronomers take for granted. It is both awe-inspiring, and a testament to his skill, perseverance, and attention to detail, that he could log the positions of the planets and stars to an unprecedented accuracy of 1 arcminute. Without the accuracy of his observational data, it would have been very difficult for Kepler to deduce the famous Kepler's Laws, that revolutionized our understanding of the universe.
