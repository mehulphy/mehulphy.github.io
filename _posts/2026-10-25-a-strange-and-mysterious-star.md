In the Diwali vacation of 2004, during the final year of my MSc, I spent a few weeks at the Astronomy and Astrophysics Division of the Physical Research Laboratory (PRL) in Ahmedabad, doing a short-term astronomy project. I was there because [Professor Somayajulu](/blog/2025/12/02/a-cross-section-of-space-time-shared-with-professor-somayajulu/) had written a recommendation letter for every student in our nuclear physics class, and, knowing my interest in the stars, he had sent me to PRL.

For a student who had only read about astronomy in books, simply walking through those corridors was exciting. But the moment I remember most is a conversation with Professor D. P. K. Banerjee.

## A star that did not follow the rules

Professor Banerjee told us about a star that he and his colleagues had been studying since it suddenly erupted in early 2002. It sat in the constellation of Monoceros, the Unicorn, and carried the name **V838 Monocerotis**, or V838 Mon for short.

In January 2002 this faint, unremarkable star brightened enormously within a few weeks. At its peak it shone with roughly a million times the luminosity of the Sun, and for a short while it was among the brightest stars in our galaxy. Naturally, the first guess was that astronomers were watching a nova, or perhaps even a kind of supernova.

But V838 Mon refused to follow the script. A nova gets hotter as it erupts and then fades back. V838 Mon did the opposite: after its outburst it swelled into an enormous, unusually cool red star. Professor Banerjee and Professor N. M. Ashok had been observing it in the infrared, and within a few months of the outburst they found its temperature had fallen to only about 2,500 degrees, cooler than almost any star we know, with molecules such as carbon monoxide and water appearing in its spectrum. No existing model of novae or supernovae explained this. I still remember the excitement in his voice: here was something the textbooks did not yet have an answer for.

The Hubble Space Telescope had photographed V838 Mon again and again after the outburst, and each image showed glowing rings that seemed to be expanding outward from the star at an impossible speed, faster than light itself. Of course, nothing was actually moving that fast. The dust around the star had been there long before 2002, quietly hidden in the dark. What we were seeing was the flash of the outburst travelling outward and lighting up that dust, layer by layer, as it reached it. Astronomers call this a **light echo**: like a sound bouncing back from distant walls, the light arrives at us later from dust that is farther away.

That idea stayed with me. The star, about 20,000 light-years away, was in a sense illuminating its own past, revealing structures that no one had ever seen and that no one would have seen without that single brief flare.

## A talk on National Science Day

I came back from PRL inspired and fully charged. Professor C. F. Desai gave me the opportunity to share what I had learned, and on National Science Day, 28 February 2005, at 5:30 in the evening, I gave a talk in room 38 of the Physics Department at the Faculty of Science, MSU Baroda. I called it *A Strange and Mysterious Star: V838 Mon*.

I began with what we understood well: how a nova happens when a white dwarf steals hydrogen from a companion star until it ignites on the surface; how a massive star builds heavier and heavier elements in its core until it reaches iron, then collapses and rebounds in a core-collapse supernova; and how a white dwarf pushed past the Chandrasekhar limit can explode in a different kind of supernova altogether. Only after that did I turn to V838 Mon, a star that fitted none of these stories. My teachers appreciated the way I explained it, and that encouragement meant a great deal to me.

The talk would not have happened without many people. Professor Banerjee discussed the topic with me and gave me valuable references. Professor Ashok kept guiding me by email while I prepared. My friend Rakesh Chauhan encouraged me to take the step and stood by me throughout. And none of it would have been possible without Professor Somayajulu, whose belief in us made the whole visit happen.

## What we know now

In 2005 V838 Mon was still a mystery. Since then, astronomers have arrived at a remarkable explanation: the outburst was most likely caused by two stars **merging** into one. Events like this are now recognised as a class of their own, called *luminous red novae*. So the instinct of those astronomers in 2002, that this star might belong to an entirely new kind of object, turned out to be right.

Looking back, what I treasure is not only the star but the way I met it. I did not learn about V838 Mon from a textbook. I learned about it from a scientist who was still working on it, who did not yet know the answer, and who was happy to share that not knowing with a curious student. It was my first real glimpse of science as something unfinished, an [unending reform](/blog/2004/10/15/unending-reforms/), and of how exciting it is to stand at the edge of what we understand.
