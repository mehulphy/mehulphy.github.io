title: "A Cross-Section of Space-⁠Time Shared with Professor Somayajulu"
description: "Remembering the teacher who believed in his students, opened the door to nuclear physics for me, and changed how I see physics and life."
categories: [reflections, physics]
---

My earliest memory of Professor Somayajulu dates back to 2004, during the "previous" year of my Master's course in Physics. At that time, he was the Head of the Department. During my BSc years, we knew him primarily through the nameplate outside his office. We would see him walking down the corridor, always dressed simply, wearing a smile that suggested he was truly happy from within. It was always a pleasure to watch him move around the department, though we lacked the courage to enter his cabin or interact with him; we were simply too shy.

My first real encounter with him happened during the "MSc previous" course when he taught us electronics. I vividly remember him teaching concepts from the Millman and Halkias electronics book. He possessed a unique ability to look into students' eyes and gauge whether they truly understood the concepts. Just by observing the way we sat or looked at the board, he could identify who was following the lesson. Since we were generally too shy to ask questions, he would sense our hesitation and voluntarily elaborate on topics he was teaching. He made the fundamentals of electronics simple and approachable, which had a profound impact on me. However, my interaction with him at that stage remained limited to the classroom.

Later, at the beginning of our MSc final year, an entrance test was conducted to determine our specializations: nuclear physics, solid-state physics, electronics, or spectroscopy. We had to take an objective exam and list our preferences in order. From my school days, I had been keenly interested in astronomy: understanding how stars are born and how supernovae explode. Consequently, nuclear physics seemed like the natural first choice.

In retrospect, I realized I had been somewhat foolish in my selection. I listed nuclear physics as my first preference, but without thinking rationally, I listed solid-state physics as my second option, followed by electronics and spectroscopy. I should have placed spectroscopy second, as it was more relevant to my interest in astronomy. When the results were declared, I had not qualified for nuclear physics and was instead granted my second choice: solid-state physics.

Realizing my mistake, I knew I needed to request a change. By this time, Professor Somayajulu was no longer the Head of the Department (Professor C.F. Desai had taken over), but he was still in charge of the specialization process. I went to his cabin and explained my situation. I told him that I had only seriously considered nuclear physics and that, in hindsight, spectroscopy should have been my proper second choice. I requested that he grant me spectroscopy instead of solid-state physics.

He looked at me and simply asked, "Why not nuclear physics?"

Caught off guard, I replied, "Sir, if I could get nuclear physics, I would be more than happy. But I understand that I didn't get it, which is why I am asking for spectroscopy."

He asked again, "Are you not interested in nuclear physics?"

I reassured him that I would be delighted to pursue it. He told me to let him think about it and come back later. When I returned, I learned that he had granted me the specialization in nuclear physics. That moment marked the beginning of a deeper journey. It was through this edge that I entered the field, and had that not happened, I likely wouldn't have had the opportunity to interact with him so closely. I remain grateful to him for considering the aspirations of his students.

This incident highlights an important quality of Professor Somayajulu: he truly believed in youth. Whenever he looked at us, I felt he was looking at the future of India. It was incredibly satisfying to know that a professor saw potential in us, which gave us the confidence to strive for more. He looked for the strengths in every student and sought ways to leverage those strengths to help them.

Our nuclear physics class consisted of nine students, and I was the ninth. Professor Somayajulu's cabin had a blackboard behind his chair. In front of his desk, there were already eight chairs arranged for the students. However, the room was just spacious enough to accommodate those eight; if a ninth chair were placed, there would be absolutely no room left to enter or navigate the space to sit down.

On the first day of class, he looked at me and asked what I would do, as the room was already at capacity. True to his jovial nature (he was always making the environment lighthearted), he quizzed me on a solution. When I awkwardly suggested I would stand, he said, "No, no. Go to the laboratory opposite and get a chair."

The lab typically only had chairs for teachers, but invoking Professor Somayajulu's name worked instantly; he was that well-respected. So, for every class, I would fetch a chair, sit near the door, and attend his lectures.

He was truly a "teacher of teachers." He understood the background knowledge we brought to the classroom, met us at that level, and gradually lifted us to the desired Master's level. He constantly connected physics with real life, using illustrations that made nuclear physics clear. More importantly, he didn't just teach a technical subject; he imparted values. He taught us how to care for society and how to be good human beings. He seemed to know that not all of us would become physicists, but he wanted to ensure that whatever field we entered, we would be good people. He fundamentally changed our outlook on life.

I also remember an outing we students planned to Pavagadh Hill. We invited him, and despite his aura of greatness, he was down-to-earth enough to join us. We enjoyed ourselves immensely. I recall a moment when we were all tired after climbing and were sitting down to rest. He pulled a piece of paper from his pocket containing quiz questions he had prepared specifically for the trip. He had us sit in a circle and engaged us in an interesting quiz. It struck me that even for a fun outing, he had invested his time and energy to ensure we learned something.

Toward the end of the year, around Diwali, he wrote recommendation letters for every student in the nuclear physics class, ensuring we all visited research institutes for projects. He was thinking ahead of his time, insisting on exposure to real research, which was rare for Master's students. Knowing my interest in astronomy, he sent me to the Astronomy and Astrophysics Division at the Physical Research Laboratory (PRL) in Ahmedabad.

It was a wonderful learning experience. I returned inspired and fully charged. I remember giving a talk on National Science Day, February 28, 2005, about V838 Monocerotis, a cataclysmic binary star in the constellation of Monoceros, which had recently undergone a significant outburst. My teachers really appreciated the way I explained the topic.

Looking back at my entire BSc and MSc journey, that one year stands out distinctively. It was a year of great inspiration. He is, simply put, a great human being: a remarkable physics teacher with an aura that instantly inspires others. Always happy and selfless, his thoughts are constantly focused on the progress of his students. Even today, it is always a pleasure to talk to him, and it remains deeply inspiring to connect with him. Professor Somayajulu touched our lives and left a permanent mark on how we view the world, how we value human qualities, and, of course, how we appreciate physics.
